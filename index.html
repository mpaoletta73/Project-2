DSC 106 · Project 2

Persuasive and Deceptive Visualizations

Two opposite views using the World Bank Human Development Indicators dataset.

Matthew Paoletta · mpaoletta@ucsd.edu Gilad Segal · gsegal@ucsd.edu Sancho Syquia · ssyquia@ucsd.edu Thomas Deitel · tdeitel@ucsd.edu
Our Proposition

Dataset: World Bank Human Development Indicators, 1960 to 2020

Investing in education is a reliable path to national prosperity.
Supporting View Higher Education Outcomes Are Associated With Greater Prosperity

Design Decisions and Rationale

1
Using tertiary enrollment as the main education outcome
Earnestness score: +1
Instead of directly comparing education spending to GDP, we used tertiary enrollment as the middle step. This made the argument more reasonable because spending on education should ideally show up in actual education outcomes, not just budget numbers. A direct spending versus GDP chart was messier and did not support the proposition as clearly. Using enrollment helped us show the education to prosperity relationship in a cleaner way.
2
Grouping countries by enrollment percentiles
Earnestness score: -0.5
We grouped countries into 10 percentile groups based on tertiary enrollment, then averaged GDP per capita inside each group. This makes the overall trend easier to see, but it also hides a lot of variation between individual countries. There is even a dip in the middle, where the 30 to 40th percentile group is lower than the 20 to 30th percentile group. Since the chart still mostly moves upward, that dip does not stand out as much as it would in a scatterplot.
3
Red to green color scale
Earnestness score: -1
The red to green color scale makes the message feel stronger right away. Red naturally feels like bad or low, while green feels like good or high. That helps the chart push the idea that higher tertiary enrollment is clearly better. A more neutral chart could have used one color, but this version makes the argument easier to understand at a glance.
4
Lowest and highest enrollment annotations
Earnestness score: -0.5
The big labels on the lowest and highest groups pull attention toward the two extremes. This makes the contrast feel more dramatic and keeps the reader focused on the biggest difference in the chart. It also makes people less likely to notice the less perfect middle part of the graph. We kept the labels because they make the supporting argument very clear.
5
Dollar labels above every bar
Earnestness score: +1
The value labels make the chart more transparent because the reader can see the exact average GDP per capita for each group. This also makes the gap between the lowest and highest enrollment groups feel more concrete. Without the labels, the chart would still show the trend, but the size of the difference would be less obvious. This is persuasive, but it is still fair because the actual numbers are shown.
Opposing View More Government Education Spending Does Not Guarantee Better Outcomes

Design Decisions and Rationale

1
Grouping countries into spending quartiles
Earnestness score: -1
For this chart, we grouped countries into four spending groups instead of showing every country as a point. This makes the overlap between groups easier to focus on, but it also hides some of the overall pattern in the raw data. If we used a scatterplot, the relationship might look more positive. The quartile grouping helps the opposing argument by making the differences between groups feel less clean.
2
Pointing out the highest spending group's median
Earnestness score: -1.5
The annotation points out that the highest spending group has a lower median than the upper middle spending group. That is true, but it is also a selective detail. The overall chart still shows that enrollment generally rises from the lower spending groups to the middle and upper groups. By highlighting this one reversal, the chart makes the relationship look less reliable than it actually might be.
3
Using violin plots to show spread
Earnestness score: -1
The violin plot puts a lot of attention on how spread out the countries are within each spending group. This helps show that countries with similar spending levels can have very different enrollment outcomes. It also makes the groups look heavily overlapping, which supports the argument that spending does not guarantee results. A simpler bar chart of medians would have made the upward pattern easier to see.
4
Muted color palette
Earnestness score: -0.5
The colors are kept pretty similar across all four groups. This makes the groups feel less different from each other, even though they are ordered from lowest to highest spending. A stronger color ramp would make the spending progression more obvious. The muted colors fit the opposing view because they make the chart feel more like these groups overlap a lot, not that the values are steadily increasing.
5
Annotation about heavy overlap
Earnestness score: -1
The annotation tells the reader to focus on the overlap between spending groups. The overlap is real, so it is not fake, but it does steer the interpretation. It pushes the reader to notice the messiness and variation instead of the fact that the middle and upper spending groups still tend to have higher enrollment. This makes the chart more persuasive against the proposition.
Final Reflection

Overall Design Process

The easiest part of the project was coming up with the general topic. Education and economic prosperity already feel connected, so the proposition made sense at first. The harder part was deciding which variables actually supported that idea. Education spending, tertiary enrollment, and GDP per capita are all related, but they do not tell the same story. The graphs changed a lot depending on what we grouped, what we averaged, and what we chose to label.

Making two opposite charts also made us think more carefully about how visualizations can persuade people. For the supporting chart, the argument became much stronger when we used tertiary enrollment instead of spending directly. The enrollment groups made the relationship with GDP look pretty clear. For the opposing chart, we focused more on government education spending and showed that spending more does not always lead to higher enrollment. That view was still based on real data, but it depended more on emphasizing overlap and exceptions.

Overall, this project showed us that persuasion and deception are not always totally separate. A chart can use real data and still push the reader toward a certain conclusion. It feels fair to use titles, colors, and annotations to make a point, as long as the reader can still understand what is being shown. It becomes more questionable when the chart hides context, focuses too much on one exception, or makes a weak pattern look stronger than it is. In our case, both charts are persuasive, but the most misleading choices are the ones that make the messy parts of the data easier to ignore.
